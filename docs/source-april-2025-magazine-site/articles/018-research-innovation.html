Solutions - Digital Monitoring New app tracks hurricane coastal flooding Researchers from the University of South Florida, USA, have used a new app to track coastal flooding during hurricanes. The web-based CRIS-HAZARD app, developed with Georgia Institute of Technology, gathers crowdsourced data to identify flooding and inform policy in coastal communities. Using dynamic modelling and mapping tools, it can identify and extract estimated water elevation from submitted imagery. In addition to the data provided for researchers, the pictures and videos are accessible to others to help keep communities informed of hazards. Currently used to analyse real-time flooding in Pinellas County, Florida, which has approximately 950 km of coastline, it is hoped that the app will be made available to communities nationwide. Water scarcity is a growing concern in Dublin, where 84% of the city’s water comes from the River Liffey, and approximately 33% of water is lost before reaching consumers. Aganova will deploy its flagship Nautilus solution - equipped with state-of-the-art sensors and Al-driven analysis - for assessing large-pressure pipes in service, providing accurate leak detection and anomaly identification with minimum disruption. Once leaks are identified, Uisce Eireann will lead the repair and maintenance efforts, ensuring the long-term sustainability of Dublin’s water supply. Aganova will continue monitoring results to assess water conservation impacts and verify the project's volumetric benefits, while SUEZ will provide operational support during implementation. Resilience Real-time monitoring to improve food security in Pakistan The Pakistan Drought Management System (PakDMS), a real-time drought monitoring and early warning system, has been launched and handed over to the Pakistan Meteorological Department (PMD) in Islamabad. Agriculture is the backbone of Pakistan's economy, employing a significant portion of its rural population. However, the sector faces significant challenges because of water scarcity, inequity in water distribution, reliance on outdated irrigation methods, inefficient water management practices, and inadequate access to reliable data for making informed decisions about water usage and crop management. This is exacerbated by climate change, which is altering weather patterns and causing extremes of floods and drought, further straining water resources. With these challenges significantly impacting the socioeconomic development of Pakistan, PakDMS, developed by the International Water Management Institute with PMD, aims to improve drought forecasting and decision-making. A team of chemists has succeeded in controlling the passage of halide ions by deliberately introducing defects into a two- layer nanographene system. Their results, published in the journal Nature, suggest new potential applications in water filtration or sensor technology. Graphene is an extremely thin, flexible and resistant material made of pure carbon. It forms layers that consist of virtually a single layer of carbon atoms. To make graphene as thick as a human hair, thousands of such layers would have to be stacked on top of each other. Scientists are interested in the benefits of controlling the permeability of graphene for different substances by creating ‘defects’ in the carbon lattice of the material. “These can be thought of as small holes that make the lattice permeable to gases,” explains Professor Frank Wiirthner, from JMU. Permeability to other substances, such as ions like fluoride, chloride or bromide, had not been observed. However, he explains that this would be of fundamental scientific interest for applications such as the desalination of water and the detection or purification of mixtures of substances. For the first time, a team led by Wirthner has now created a model system with a defect that allows the halides fluoride, chloride and bromide to pass through, but not iodide. This was achieved ina stable double layer consisting of two nanographenes that encloses a cavity. The penetrated halide ions are bound in this cavity so that the time required for entry can be measured. “The proof of a high permeability for chloride by single-layer nanographene and a selective binding of halides in a double-layer of nanographene brings some applications closer,” says Dr Kazutaka Shoyama, who initiated and led the project together with Wiirthner. Such applications include water filtration membranes, artificial receptors and chloride channels. The study, published in Solar Energy, is believed to be the first to consider which water sources have the right conditions to support these kinds of power plants, The study takes into consideration shipping traffic, which could cause wakes, damaging mooring lines and impacting floating infrastructure. Other considerations include waterbodies that are too cold and reservoirs that are too shallow or have sloping bottoms that are too steep to secure solar panels in place. The study identifies some hydropower reservoirs that could be ideal locations for floating solar power plants, creating a hybrid energy system that uses both solar energy and hydropower to provide a more reliable and resilient energy source to the power grid. Future plans for the project include a review to identify which reservoirs are located close to transmission lines, the local electricity demands, the potential costs for development at specific sites, environmental considerations, and how developers might navigate state and federal regulations. The team would also like to evaluate more potential locations, including smaller reservoirs, estuaries, and even ocean sites. Research & Innovation US Environmental Security programme tests new PFAS technology Revive Environmental Technology (Revive Environmental) and Battelle have been selected to participate in a Department of Defense Environmental Security Technology Certification Program project contracted through the Defense Innovation Unit, USA. The programme aims to demonstrate a first-of-its-kind technology capable of destroying toxic per- and polyfluoroalky! substances (PFAS), eliminating pollution to the environment. The field demonstration will showcase Revive Environmental’s PFAS Annihilator at a Clean Earth hazardous waste service facility, in Detroit, USA.
