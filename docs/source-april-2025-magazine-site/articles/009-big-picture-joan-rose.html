IWA Global Water Award得主Joan Rose從水媒病原體、水質劣化與全球公共衛生角度，呼籲以科學、監測與知識共享因應安全飲水與衛生挑戰。

中文全文翻譯

# 觀點：Joan Rose 的大視野 全球正面臨多重交織的水問題，危及人類健康與環境健康。知名科學家、IWA 全球水獎得主 Joan Rose 教授，將其知識帶入水務部門所面對的尖銳議題。Erika Yarrow-Soden 採訪。 Rose 教授是全球研究隱孢子蟲（Cryptosporidium）的權威，也是第一位提出在供水中偵測此病原體方法的人。她因在理解水質與保護公共衛生方面做出突破性進展而獲得肯定。 她是廢水監測領域的全球領導者，現任美國密西根州立大學 Homer Nowlin 水研究講座教授及該校水資源聯盟主任。她的研究涵蓋水再利用、病原體去除、病原體來源追蹤，以及氣候與水媒疾病；她對水質如何影響健康的理解作出貢獻，並因此於 2024 年獲得 IWA 最高全球榮譽──全球水獎。 回顧職涯起點，她記得當時認為水質，尤其是美國水質，是良好的。「我在 1980 年代進入這個領域，當時剛好出版了水相關疾病風險與介入措施的基準性研究。」 這份出版品《衛生與疾病：排泄物與廢水管理的健康面向》（Sanitation and Disease: Health Aspects of Excreta and Wastewater Management，Feachem 等人，1983），後來啟發她與團隊發起全球水病原體計畫（Global Water Pathogens Project，GWPP）。這項計畫致力發展知識資源、引導未來實現安全用水的目標、協助降低與水病原體相關的死亡率，並藉由建立、出版與傳播可取代上述水相關疾病風險與介入措施基準出版品的最先進資源，回應全球缺乏安全飲用水與基本衛生服務的問題。 ## 全球水質 「那是個非常適合投入這個研究領域的時期，因為它受到高度關注，」她接著說。「然後有千禧年發展目標（MDGs），它們非常具有願景性，接著是永續發展目標（SDGs）。但現在我們似乎離達成它們還很遠，情勢確實正在崩解。」 Rose 談到全球水環境的劣化。「面臨風險的海岸線、河流與湖泊公里數更多；有更多有害藻華、更多糞便污染。因此，水質日漸惡化的趨勢，確實是行星健康的危機。」 基於這樣的理解，她非常憂心對人類健康的影響。她承認，監測水質能為生活在世界某些地區的人群提供部分保護，但仍存在許多未知，包括新興污染物與地下水水質。 這項複雜挑戰還因全球人口增加與動物數量上升而加劇；兩者皆增加環境中的糞便負荷，危及健康，特別是在缺乏安全供水與廢水處理的世界地區。 「我們對動物族群，尤其野生動物的健康，所知很少。禽流感就是疾病可能透過野生動物傳播的完美例子。但我們甚至不知道是否存在一條標準傳播途徑；雖然我們相信它可能經由糞便排出並透過氣膠傳播。 「我們也知道牛隻有些疾病會感染人類。但直到如今面對這場危機，我們才認知到知識上存在缺口，以及那些我們看不見、也尚未計算的潛在污染風險。」 ## 人為介入 Rose 所辨識的其他「大視野」挑戰，還包括人類對景觀造成的改變：農業與都市化導致濕地流失、污染負荷增加，以及其中最全面的挑戰──氣候變遷。世界正經歷氣候與降水的高度變異，洪水與乾旱增加。 她說：「這些全球性的行星變化正在改變我們的水質。我甚至不認為我們真正理解這些變化中的許多部分，也不知道我們必須做什麼，才能開始保護仍擁有的事物，並修復已經劣化的部分。」 不過，Rose 強調，我們確實有資料清楚顯示，強降水事件發生時，疾病暴發會增加。 ## COVID-19 疫情 另一項全球挑戰是 COVID-19 疫情。它使水，尤其是廢水，受到關注，並引起人們對透過監測廢水了解社區健康狀況之可能性的興趣。Rose 說：「這或許是烏雲背後的一線曙光。」 「但疫情對我們的經濟與基礎設施投資造成很大壓力，導致進展延遲。我們知道，這對低收入與中等收入國家更為艱難；即使在高收入國家，低收入與中等收入社區亦然。那是壓力與衝擊最嚴重的地方。」 雖然擔心疫情對進展的影響，Rose 對疫情帶來的廢水關注仍感到些許安慰，特別是它帶動儀器設備與檢測套組市場的興趣；此前私部門並未認為這是一個可行市場。 ## 社會政治動態 當世界緩慢從疫情中復原，全球政治動態正造成額外壓力。她說：「當我們開始看到人民感到不安，且人們對自己的未來道路缺乏希望時，環境就開始被擱置一旁，成為較低的優先事項。」她並指出，研究顯示，在繁榮、希望與和平程度較高的地方，人們更重視環境。 她提到 Peter Gleick 近作《水的三個時代：史前的過去、受威脅的現在與未來的希望》（The Three Ages of Water: Prehistoric Past, Imperilled Present, and a Hope for the Future）。該書指出，水在過去被視為和平的平台，但資料如今顯示，過去 20 年間因水而起的衝突日益增加。 Rose 說：「我們正處於歷史上一個奇特的位置，所有這些事情同時匯聚。對水務工作者而言，這表示我們必須思考韌性，並持續做我們知道正確的事。」她的關切不限於人類健康與社區經濟──我們知道投資水務基礎設施可改善社區經濟。她也擔心例如資源回收等創新技術的命運，以及使其潛能成真的必要投資。 ## 為政策提供資訊 那麼，學者能做什麼，確保研究發現反映在帶來具體改變的政策中？Rose 回答：「我相信文字的力量。我認為，身為科學家，我們必須持續發表研究。你永遠不知道哪一篇出版品會被誰注意到；我們需要盡可能好地傳達工作。政策的關鍵在於持續堅持──成為持續發聲的人，持續而一致。」 Rose 在取得博士學位之前便加入後來稱為 IWA 的組織，她說自己很幸運在職涯早期「找到了自己的組織」。 她表示：「在我的整個科學生涯裡，我一直是這個組織的一部分。它演進、它改變，我們也改過名稱；而當我們談韌性時，IWA 這類專業組織將扮演極其重要的角色。」 Rose 強調，必須讓青年專業人士參與，並在其整個職涯中於組織內支持他們。她總結道：「這就是差異所在。當擁有這些聲音──共享友誼與信任──你就能成為別人所信賴的聲音。」 ## Global water quality “This was a great time to be in this area of research, because it was receiving so much attention,” she continues. “Then we had the MDGs [Millennium Development Goals], which were very aspirational, and then the SDGs [Sustainable Development Goals]. But now we seem to be far from reaching them and things are really falling apart.” Joan refers to the degradation of water globally. “We have more kilometres of shoreline, rivers and lakes at risk. We have more hazardous algal blooms, more faecal pollution. So this growing trend of the degradation of water quality is really a crisis for planetary health.” From this understanding she has real concern for the impact this is having on human health - acknowledging that while monitoring water quality provides some protection to populations living in some parts of the world, 66 Global planetary changes are changing our water quality Professor Joan Rose receiving IWA’s Global Water Award last year from then IWA President, Tom Mollenkopf AO there are still many unknowns, including emerging contaminants and groundwater quality. Adding to this complex challenge, Joan makes reference to the growing global population and increasing animal population, all adding to the faecal load to the environment and endangering health, especially in parts of the world without safe water and wastewater treatment. “We know very little about the health of our animal population, in particular, our wildlife. Bird flu is a perfect example of how disease can be spread through wildlife. But we don’t even know if there is a standard route of transmission, although we believe it may be excreted through faeces and transmitted through aerosols. “We also know that there are diseases in cattle that can infect humans. But it is only now, as we face this crisis, that we are recognising there are gaps in our knowledge and potential contamination risks that we don’t see and haven’t calculated.” Human interventions Further ‘big picture’ challenges that Joan identifies are the changes that we have made to our landscape, including the loss of wetlands because of agriculture and 66 Bea persistent voice urbanisation, increasing pollutant loads, and the most all-encompassing challenge of them all - climate change. The world is experiencing high variations in climate and precipitation, increasing floods and drought. “These global planetary changes are changing our water quality,” she says. “I don’t think we even understand much of these changes or what we are going to have to do to start protecting what we have and start restoring what we have already degraded.” Joan highlights that we do, however, have data that clearly shows that outbreaks of disease increase when there are high precipitation events. COVID-19 pandemic An additional global challenge was the COVID-19 pandemic. This put a spotlight on water, especially wastewater, and drew interest to the possibility of learning about the health of a community by monitoring its wastewater. “This may be the silver lining,” says Joan. “But the pandemic put a big strain on our economics and our investments in infrastructure, creating delays to progress. And we know that this was harder on low-and middle-income countries. Even our low-and middle-income communities in high-income countries. That’s where the high stress and impact has been.” Although concerned about the impact of the pandemic on progress, Joan takes some solace in the heightened interest in wastewater that it created - in particular, in the market for instrumentation and testing kits for which, previously, the private sector had not seen a viable market. Sociopolitical dynamics While the world is slowly recovering from the pandemic, global political dynamics are creating additional strains. “When we start to see feelings of unrest coming from the people and there is a lack of hope from people about their own pathways, the environment starts to go by the wayside. The environment becomes a lower priority,” she says, highlighting that studies have shown that people prioritise the environment in places where there is greater prosperity, hope and peace. She refers to Peter Gleick’s recent book, The Three Ages of Water: Prehistoric Past, Imperilled Present, and a Hope for the Future, which highlights that, while in the past water was seen as a platform for peace, data now shows that there has been increasing conflict over water in the past 20 years. “We are ina strange place in history right now, where all these things are coming together. For those of us in the water business, it means we have to think about resilience and keep moving on with what we know is right,” says Joan. Her concern is not limited to human health and the economics of communities, which we know is improved by investments in water infrastructure. She is also concerned about the fate of innovative technologies for resource recovery, for example, and the investment that will be required to make their potential a reality. Informing policy So, what can academics do to ensure their findings are reflected in policies that will bring about tangible change? “I believe in the written word,” answers Joan. “I think, as scientists, we need to keep publishing our work. You never know when a publication is going to be picked up by someone: And we need to communicate our work as best we can. The thing about policy is that you have to stay persistent — be a persistent voice — persistent and consistent.” Joining what is now known as IWA before she got her PhD, Joan says she was fortunate to “find her organisation” early in her career. “IT have been part of this organisation my whole scientific life,” she says. “It’s evolved, it’s changed, we’ve changed names — and when we talk about resilience - professional organisations such as IWA are going to play an extremely important role.” Emphasising the importance of engaging young professionals and supporting them within the organisation throughout their professional life, Joan concludes, “That’s what makes the difference.
