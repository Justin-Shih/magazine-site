Global water quality “This was a great time to be in this area of research, because it was receiving so much attention,” she continues. “Then we had the MDGs [Millennium Development Goals], which were very aspirational, and then the SDGs [Sustainable Development Goals]. But now we seem to be far from reaching them and things are really falling apart.” Joan refers to the degradation of water globally. “We have more kilometres of shoreline, rivers and lakes at risk. We have more hazardous algal blooms, more faecal pollution. So this growing trend of the degradation of water quality is really a crisis for planetary health.” From this understanding she has real concern for the impact this is having on human health - acknowledging that while monitoring water quality provides some protection to populations living in some parts of the world, 66 Global planetary changes are changing our water quality 26 | The Source | April 2025 Professor Joan Rose receiving IWA’s Global Water Award last year from then IWA President, Tom Mollenkopf AO there are still many unknowns, including emerging contaminants and groundwater quality. Adding to this complex challenge, Joan makes reference to the growing global population and increasing animal population, all adding to the faecal load to the environment and endangering health, especially in parts of the world without safe water and wastewater treatment. “We know very little about the health of our animal population, in particular, our wildlife. Bird flu is a perfect example of how disease can be spread through wildlife. But we don’t even know if there is a standard route of transmission, although we believe it may be excreted through faeces and transmitted through aerosols. “We also know that there are diseases in cattle that can infect humans. But it is only now, as we face this crisis, that we are recognising there are gaps in our knowledge and potential contamination risks that we don’t see and haven’t calculated.” Human interventions Further ‘big picture’ challenges that Joan identifies are the changes that we have made to our landscape, including the loss of wetlands because of agriculture and 66 Bea persistent voice urbanisation, increasing pollutant loads, and the most all- encompassing challenge of them all - climate change. The world is experiencing high variations in climate and precipitation, increasing floods and drought. “These global planetary changes are changing our water quality,” she says. “I don’t think we even understand much of these changes or what we are going to have to do to start protecting what we have and start restoring what we have already degraded.” Joan highlights that we do, however, have data that clearly shows that outbreaks of disease increase when there are high precipitation events. COVID-19 pandemic An additional global challenge was the COVID-19 pandemic. This put a spotlight on water, especially wastewater, and drew interest to the possibility of learning about the health of a community by monitoring its wastewater. “This may be the silver lining,” says Joan. “But the pandemic put a big strain on our economics and our investments in infrastructure, creating delays to progress. And we know that this was harder on low- and middle-income countries. Even our low- and middle-income communities in high-income countries. That’s where the high stress and impact has been.” Although concerned about the impact of the pandemic on progress, Joan takes some solace in the heightened interest in wastewater that it created - in particular, in the market for instrumentation and testing kits for which, previously, the private sector had not seen a viable market. Sociopolitical dynamics While the world is slowly recovering from the pandemic, global political dynamics are creating additional strains. “When we start to see feelings of unrest coming from the people and there is a lack of hope from people about their own pathways, the environment starts to go by the wayside. The environment becomes a lower priority,” she says, highlighting that studies have shown that people prioritise the environment in places where there is greater prosperity, hope and peace. She refers to Peter Gleick’s recent book, The Three Ages of Water: Prehistoric Past, Imperilled Present, and a Hope for the Future, which highlights that, while in the past water was seen as a platform for peace, data now shows that there has been increasing conflict over water in the past 20 years. “We are ina strange place in history right now, where all these things are coming together. For those of us in the water business, it means we have to think about resilience and keep moving on with what we know is right,” says Joan. Her concern is not limited to human health and the economics of communities, which we know is improved by investments in water infrastructure. She is also concerned about the fate of innovative technologies for resource recovery, for example, and the investment that will be required to make their potential a reality. Informing policy So, what can academics do to ensure their findings are reflected in policies that will bring about tangible change? “I believe in the written word,” answers Joan. “I think, as scientists, we need to keep publishing our work. You never know when a publication is going to be picked up by someone: And we need to communicate our work as best we can. The thing about policy is that you have to stay persistent — be a persistent voice — persistent and consistent.” Joining what is now known as IWA before she got her PhD, Joan says she was fortunate to “find her organisation” early in her career. “IT have been part of this organisation my whole scientific life,” she says. “It’s evolved, it’s changed, we’ve changed names — and when we talk about resilience - professional organisations such as IWA are going to play an extremely important role.” Emphasising the importance of engaging young professionals and supporting them within the organisation throughout their professional life, Joan concludes, “That’s what makes the difference.
