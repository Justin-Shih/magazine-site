中文全文翻譯

泰國曼谷舉行的國際水協會(IWA, International Water Association)水與發展大會暨展覽(Water and Development Congress & Exhibition)，以「水、衛生與創新：通往進步與韌性未來的路徑」為主題。這個主題反映水議題具有多重面向，相關關切既多元又彼此交疊；水行動所帶來的效益，也會沿著相互連結的脈絡回到人類與自然世界。在這一切之中，氣候變遷日益受到關注，而妥善管理的水正是韌性的基礎。 因此，當水務界觀察《聯合國氣候變化綱要公約》(UNFCCC, United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change)締約方大會(COP, Conference of the Parties)進程持續推動氣候行動時，我們知道氣候議題會增加壓力，卻不會完全定義我們的工作議程，因為仍有許多其他工作必須完成。不過，長期圖像已足夠清楚：氣候行動必須被納入本領域的制度與日常運作之中。 這個長期圖像以溫室氣體(GHGs, greenhouse gases)排放前景為基礎；顯然地，還有更多工作必須盡快完成。聯合國環境規劃署(UNEP, United Nations Environment Programme；見新聞頁)以定期發布的《排放差距報告》(Emissions Gap Report)提供持續的基準。它最重要的警訊是：全球平均溫度很可能在未來十年內升至較工業化前高1.5°C，而若要限制超越此門檻的幅度，甚至要讓2100年回到較工業化前高1.5°C成為可能，就必須更快且更大幅度地減少溫室氣體排放。 這只是近幾個月大量報告中的一項。這些報告持續對氣候變遷的直接衝擊提出警告，也指出許多更廣泛的問題可能因氣候改變而加劇。 其中最受矚目的是世界銀行首度發布的《全球水資源監測報告》(Global Water Monitoring Report)。該報告提供目前最詳細的全球淡水減少圖像，警告世界每年正流失3,240億立方公尺淡水。報告指出，損失不只源自日益惡化的乾旱，也來自不可持續的土地與水資源作法，從不當訂價政策到過度灌溉皆包括在內。 聯合國糧食及農業組織(FAO, Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations)也在最新報告中警告，一場「無聲危機」式的土地退化正在威脅許多人的福祉。報告指出，約17億人居住在因人為土地退化而導致作物產量失敗的地區。鹽化與不可持續灌溉等自然和人為因素都在其中發揮作用。 全球森林前景同樣令人憂心。聯合國歐洲經濟委員會(UNECE, United Nations Economic Commission for Europe)指出，與氣溫上升有關的野火及乾旱在全球持續增加；它也提出其他擔憂，認為全球森林可能遭到無法修復的破壞與過度利用。 氣候間的連結雖然複雜，但必須形成把行動嵌入更廣泛計畫的議程，這一點已十分明確。正如曼谷大會的架構用以涵蓋氣候需求，明年的世界水大會暨展覽(World Water Congress & Exhibition)也將以「水行動：通往韌性與繁榮的道路」為主題舉行。 Keith Hayward，編輯

Editorial IWA’s Water and Development Congress & Exhibition in Bangkok, Thailand, was staged under the theme of ‘Water, sanitation, and innovation — pathways to progress and a resilient future’. The theme captures the sense that water is multifaceted, the concerns around it diverse and overlapping, and the gains from action on water returned to the human and natural world in interlinked strands. And within all of that, there are the rising concerns around climate change and the need for well-managed water as a foundation of resilience. So, as the water sector looks on at the ongoing efforts of the COP process around the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change to advance action on climate change, we know it is an issue that adds pressure but does not fully define our agenda, as there is so much else to be done. But at the same time, the long-term picture is clear enough: that climate action needs to be baked into the fabric of our sector. That long-term picture is grounded in the prospects for greenhouse gas emissions (GHGs) where, quite clearly, much more needs to be done and done soon. The United Nations Environment Programme (see News) provides an ongoing benchmark in this respect — through its regular Emissions Gap Report. Its top-line warning signal is that a global temperature of 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels is very likely to be reached within the next decade, and that faster and bigger reductions in GHGs are needed to limit overshoot above this and for a return to 1.5°C above pre- industrial levels by 2100 to even be a possibility. This is one of a slew of reports (see News) over recent months that continue to raise concerns around direct impacts of climate change and of wider issues that are zem Integrating action on the climate agenda at risk of being exacerbated by a changing climate. Prominent among these is the first edition of the Global Water Monitoring Report released by the World Bank. Providing the most detailed picture yet of global freshwater decline, it warns that the world is losing 324 billion m? of freshwater every year. According to the report, these losses are driven not only by worsening droughts but also by unsustainable land and water practices, ranging from poor pricing policies to excessive irrigation. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization has warned in its latest report of a ‘silent crisis’ of land degradation that is threatening the welfare of many. The report warns that around 1.7 billion people are living in areas where crop yields are failing because of human-induced land degradation. Natural and human factors are at play, including salinisation and unsustainable irrigation practices. There are also concerns around prospects for the world’s forests. The UN Economic Commission for Europe has highlighted the rising number of wildfires and droughts globally linked to warmer temperatures. It also raises other concerns, with fears that the world’s forests risk being damaged and overexploited beyond repair. So, while the climate connections can be complex, the need to forge agendas that embed action as part of broader plans is clear. And just as the Bangkok Congress was framed to capture the climate need, so too will next year’s World Water Congress & Exhibition, which will be held under the theme of ‘Water action — the path to resilience and prosperity’. Keith Hayward, Editor
