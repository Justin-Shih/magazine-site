﻿## 我們是否忘了為何要做衛生？ 隨著標籤、概念和服務模式不斷出現，Kala Vairavamoorthy 主張，衛生部門不能忘記衛生本身的根本目的。 我讀公共衛生工程時，有一堂課被一位教授反覆灌輸給我：在討論技術之前，在討論基礎設施之前，在討論設計與規格之前，我們總是被鼓勵先問一個簡單問題：「我們為什麼要做這件事？」答案從來不是「為了建造供水系統」、「為了建下水道」，或「為了安裝衛生技術」。這些都只是達成目的的手段。真正的目標始終是改善人們的生活品質。 然而，對公共衛生工程師而言，公共健康成果提供了一個特別有力的視角。理解疾病如何傳播，以及我們的介入如何阻斷這些途徑，讓我們能以一種務實方式判斷：我們是否真的在人的生活中創造有意義的改變。 為了幫助我們如此思考，我們學習 David Bradley 的疾病傳播途徑框架。Bradley 鼓勵我們不要從技術開始，而是從疾病如何傳播開始。水媒疾病、水洗不足相關疾病、水生疾病，以及與水相關的昆蟲媒介疾病，各自指向不同的介入策略。無論後來出現多少修正，這個框架都提供了一種有力思考方式，幫助我們理解介入措施、暴露途徑與公共健康成果之間的關係。 真正留在我心中的，不只是框架本身，而是它鼓勵的一種思考紀律。它沒有把衛生簡化為基礎設施，而是提供一種有結構的方式來思考因果關係，把介入視為更大系統的一部分，並始終聚焦於那些我們最終想改善其生活的人。 每項介入都必須回答兩個問題。第一，我們正在阻斷哪一條傳播途徑？第二，我們可能在系統其他地方造成什麼非預期後果？第二個問題常被忽略。公共衛生工程中充滿這樣的例子：解決一個問題，可能無意中創造另一個問題。一套保護使用者的衛生系統，可能讓工作者暴露在風險中；一項排水介入可能解決一處風險，卻在下游創造另一個風險；一項節水解方可能節省資源，卻在服務鏈其他地方引入健康風險。用系統視角看介入，有助於在這些取捨變成問題之前先看見它們。 這種對非預期後果的重視，貫穿了我的整個職涯。介入措施不會孤立運作；理解傳播途徑並進行系統性思考，是任何想改善人們生活的人都必須具備的基本紀律。這也正是我希望剛進入我們部門的青年水專業人士能持續掌握的能力。它們不是應該被淘汰的老派基礎，而是能讓他們的貢獻真正重要的思考方式。大約 35 年後，我仍不斷回到這些課題，也越來越常思考：我們的部門是否給了它們應有的重視？ 我提出這點時有些遲疑，因為今天的衛生部門充滿聰明的人、創新的技術和雄心勃勃的想法。然而，我常常在會議結束後帶著越來越強的不安離開。不是因為我們缺乏想法，而是因為我們似乎花越來越多時間討論要怎麼稱呼某些事情，卻花較少時間討論我們為什麼要做這些事。 我參加過無數會議，會中圍繞術語出現熱烈辯論，人們投入大量精力定義新概念、調整標籤，並建立不同學派之間的區別。新術語出現，新框架被提出，新運動累積動能。但有時我會問自己：我們是否正在失去對原本問題的注意力？我們一開始究竟是想解決什麼？ 這樣的反思不是出於犬儒，也不是要批評那些我深深尊重其工作的同事。相反地，它來自真正的關切。我不是以局外人的身分寫下這些話。多年來，我參與過許多關於新衛生方法的討論，也曾自己撰文和演講，主張需要挑戰傳統假設並保持對新想法的開放。我仍然相信，創新、多元解方和新思維是必要的。甚至可以說，這篇反思同樣是在對我自己說，也是對整個部門說。 在許多這類討論中，我常想到 Jeffrey Pfeffer 和 Robert I. Sutton 在《Knowing-Doing Gap》中提到的一句話：「把知識轉化為行動的主要障礙之一，是人們傾向於把談論某件事，當成真的在處理那件事。」這句話一直留在我心裡，因為它捕捉到我在我們部門偶爾感受到的狀態。問題不是我們缺乏知識，而是我們有時把命名和描述某件事，與真正處理它混為一談。 我們非常擅長談論衛生。但我們是否同樣擅長保持對衛生根本目的的專注？我們是否始終清楚自己試圖阻斷哪些傳播途徑？我們是否在問，我們的介入是否真正改善人們的生活？或者，我們是否越來越忙於辯論標籤，而底層的公共健康挑戰仍然沒有改變？ ### 從問題開始，而不是從技術開始 Bradley 框架的一大優點，是它迫使我們從問題開始，而不是從解方開始。無論問題是受污染的飲用水、個人衛生可及不足，還是媒介相關疾病，介入都應該跟隨診斷而來。這個框架迫使實務工作者思考功能，而不是思考基礎設施。廁所、下水道和處理廠本身從來不是目標；它們只是改善公共健康成果的手段。 也許這聽起來很明顯，但我越來越懷疑，我們是否仍有意識地套用這種思考方式。今天，我們許多討論都從技術、服務模式或衛生類別開始。我們討論 onsite sanitation、faecal sludge management、non-sewered sanitation、water-efficient sanitation、circular sanitation、regenerative sanitation 和 next-generation sanitation systems。這些概念並非沒有原因而出現。糞污管理凸顯了我們對收容之後發生什麼事理解上的關鍵缺口，迫使我們思考收集、運輸、處理和處置。節水衛生則回應了對水資源短缺的正當關切。這些概念中許多確實對部門做出正面貢獻。問題不是它們存在；問題是我們是否變得比起目的，更專注於術語。 ### 當標籤變成身份 隨著時間推移，標籤可能不再只是描述工具。它們會變成身份，變成實務社群，變成運動，並圍繞它們形成資金流、會議和專業網絡。這些事情本身不一定不好。挑戰出現在討論開始圍繞捍衛或推廣特定標籤，而不是解決底層問題。 有時候，我感覺我們花了大量精力辯論一套衛生系統應該被稱為這個或那個，卻較少關注它是否真的降低了人們暴露於病原體的風險。在這些討論中，我有時心情沉重。不是因為我反對創新，也不是因為我抗拒改變，而是因為我偶爾會懷疑，我們是否把運動誤認為進步。 令人不舒服的現實是，病原體並不在乎我們使用什麼術語。它們不參加會議，不讀策略計畫，也不認識捐助者優先事項。它們不在乎一個專案被歸類為 onsite sanitation、faecal sludge management、non-sewered sanitation、water-efficient sanitation 或 next-generation sanitation。它們只在乎一件事：它們用來傳播的途徑是否仍然開放。 我看不出為什麼糞污管理、非下水道衛生、節水衛生、全城包容性衛生和其他方法的專家，不能各自貢獻有價值的東西。他們往往是在看同一個挑戰的不同面向：服務鏈、基礎設施選擇、資源效率、規劃與治理。這些視角不一定互相矛盾。在許多情況下，它們應該彼此強化。然而，有些討論的展開方式卻暗示了不同情況。 新術語常常帶著一種隱含假設，好像過去的框架是不完整、過時或不足的。對話可能逐漸從理解不同觀點如何有助於解決共同問題，轉向判定哪一種框架應該占主導。當這種情況發生時，術語就不再像理解工具，而更像互相競爭的意識形態。 我個人覺得這令人不安，因為公共健康問題很少能由單一視角解決。疾病傳播是複雜的，人類行為是複雜的，服務交付也是複雜的。因此，任何單一標籤無論多有用，都不太可能完整捕捉所有面向。也許我們的目標不應該是決定哪個術語勝出，而是理解每個視角對改善健康和生活品質這個更大目標有何貢獻。 ### 新標籤是否增加了價值？ 我們應該如何判斷一個新的衛生概念或標籤是否真正增加了價值？ 有些確實如此。糞污管理也許是最清楚的例子。它不只是引入新語言，而是把注意力、資源和技術努力導向衛生服務鏈中長期被系統性忽略的一部分。它改變了實務工作者看的東西、資助者優先支持的東西，最終也改變了實際建造的東西。術語之所以重要，是因為實務跟著改變了。 這或許就是任何新概念的檢驗標準。它是否幫助我們更好地理解問題？是否幫助我們發展更好的解方？是否改善我們降低暴露並提升人們生活品質的能力？或者，它主要只是改變我們描述問題時使用的語言？ 我知道，也有人會合理地主張，術語清晰往往是實務進展的前提；如果沒有共同語言，資源無法對齊，政策無法撰寫，實務工作者也無法合作。我不否定這個論點。但語言清晰和目的清晰不是同一件事。問題在於，新語言是否讓我們更接近後者。 這些問題很重要，因為根本挑戰沒有改變。我們如何安全地把人與病原體隔開、阻斷疾病傳播並改善生活品質？這些問題在最新術語出現之前就存在，也會在它被下一個新詞取代之後繼續存在。 我的擔憂不是新想法出現，而是我們有時把新語言的出現，誤認為新理解的達成。當這種情況發生時，我們就有風險以比實務進展更快的速度創造概念複雜性。 ### 回到第一原理 這並不是反對創新的論點。創新仍然必要。今天衛生面臨的挑戰非常巨大。都市化、氣候變遷、水資源短缺、資源限制和不平等，都要求新的思考。重點是，創新不應該要求我們放棄最初賦予衛生介入正當性的原則。 因此，每當一個新概念、框架或技術出現時，也許我們都應該問幾個簡單問題。它阻斷了哪些傳播途徑？它可能造成哪些非預期後果？它是否改善人們的生活品質？如果我們無法清楚回答這些問題，就有風險讓語言演進得比實務更快。 Bradley 框架的價值不在於它提供了所有答案，而在於它迫使我們從第一原理開始，從疾病傳播出發，再走向介入。如今，我越來越擔心我們有時正好相反：我們從技術、概念和標籤開始，再往回推。這個差異看似細微，但它很重要。 ### 一個簡單提醒 也許我真正主張的只是這件事：我們要記得自己為什麼做我們正在做的事。 衛生從根本上說，不是關於技術、系統或標籤。這些東西重要，但它們不是目標。目標始終如一：降低暴露、保護健康、改善生活品質。其他一切都是達成這個目標的手段。 身為學生時，這個教訓被一位希望學生像公共衛生工程師、而不只是基礎設施設計師那樣思考的教授反覆灌輸給我。回頭看，我越來越感謝那堂課。因為在一個術語不斷擴張的世界中，它仍然提供了一項極其簡單的測試。每當我們發現自己在辯論標籤時，也許應該停下來問：這如何幫助人們過更健康的生活？ 我會說，這是我對我們所有人的挑戰，尤其是對那些將形塑我們部門未來的青年水專業人士。你們帶來部門所需的創新和企圖心；我希望你們能把這些與持久的第一原理結合起來。 下一次我們遇到新的概念、框架或術語時，讓我們保持好奇，探索它能教我們什麼，並繼續對新想法和新視角保持開放。但我們也要有紀律與勇氣，回到第一原理，問清楚我們正在試圖解決什麼問題，正在試圖阻斷哪些傳播途徑，以及眼前這個想法將如何真正改善人們的生活。不是在理論上，而是在實務中；不是抽象的人，而是真實的人。 如果我們能做到這一點，那麼我們部門中的許多不同視角也許不必彼此競爭。相反地，它們可以成為互補的鏡頭，幫助我們更好地理解一項複雜挑戰，並最終實現那個始終最重要的目標：改善我們所服務人群的健康、尊嚴與生活品質。因為如果我們失去對這個目標的關注，那麼也許我們真的忘了衛生最初是為了什麼。 作者 Kala Vairavamoorthy 是 International Water Association 執行長。

Sanitation Sanitation Have we forgotten why we do sanitation? As labels, concepts and service models continue to emerge, Kala Vairavamoorthy argues that the sanitation sector must not lose sight of the fundamental purpose of sanitation itself. hen I was studying public health engineering one lesson was drummed into me repeatedly by one of my professors — before discussing technologies, before discussing infrastructure, before discussing designs and specifications, we were always encouraged to ask a simple question: ‘Why are we doing this?” The answer was never ‘to build a water supply system’, ‘to construct a sewer’, or ‘to install a sanitation technology’. Those things were simply a means to an end. The real objective was always to improve people's quality of life. Yet for public health engineers, public health outcomes provided a particularly powerful lens. Understanding how disease spreads, and how our interventions interrupted those pathways, gave us a practical way of judging whether we were making a meaningful difference in people’s lives. To help us think this way, we were taught David Bradley's framework of disease transmission routes. Rather than starting with technologies, Bradley encouraged us to start with how disease spreads. Water- borne diseases, water-washed diseases, water-based diseases and water-related insect-vector diseases each pointed towards different intervention strategies. Whatever refinements have emerged since then, the framework provided a powerful way of thinking about the relationship between interventions, exposure pathways and public health outcomes. What stayed with me was not simply the framework itself, but the discipline of thinking it encouraged. Rather than reducing sanitation to infrastructure, it provided a structured way of thinking about cause and effect, viewing interventions as part of a wider system, focused always on the people whose lives we were ultimately trying to improve. Every intervention had to answer two questions. First, which transmission route are we interrupting? Second, what unintended consequences might we create elsewhere in the system? That second question is often overlooked. Public health engineering is full of examples where solving one problem can inadvertently create another. A sanitation system that protects users may expose workers. A drainage intervention may solve one risk while creating another downstream. A water-efficient solution may conserve resources while introducing health risks elsewhere in the chain. Looking at interventions through a systems lens helps reveal these trade-offs before they become problems. This emphasis on unintended consequences has stayed with me throughout my career. Interventions do not operate in isolation and understanding transmission pathways and thinking systemically are fundamental disciplines for anyone seeking to improve people’s lives. These are exactly the disciplines I would urge the young water professionals entering our sector to hold onto — not as old-fashioned fundamentals to be outgrown, but as the very thinking that will make their contributions matter. Some 35 or so years later, I still find myself returning to those lessons, and increasingly I wonder whether our sector is giving them the attention they deserve. Isay this with some hesitation because the sanitation sector today is full of intelligent people, innovative technologies and ambitious ideas. Yet I often leave meetings with a growing sense of unease, not because we lack ideas, but because we seem to spend an increasing amount of time discussing what to call things and less time discussing why we are doing them. Thave sat through countless meetings where passionate debates emerge around terminology, where considerable effort is spent defining new concepts, refining labels and establishing distinctions between different schools of thought. New terms emerge, new frameworks are proposed, and new movements gather momentum. But sometimes I find myself wondering whether we are losing sight of the original problem. we were trying to solve. This reflection does not come from. a place of cynicism, nor is it intended as criticism of colleagues whose work I respect deeply. Rather, it comes from. a place of genuine concern. Ido not write this as an outsider to these debates. Over the years, I have contributed to many discussions around new sanitation approaches and have myself written and spoken about the need to challenge conventional assumptions and remain open to new ideas. I continue to believe that innovation, diversity of solutions and fresh thinking are essential. If anything, this reflection is directed as much at myself as it is at the sector. In many of these discussions, I find myself thinking of a quote by Jeffrey Pfeffer and Robert I Sutton in their work on the ‘Knowing- Doing Gap’: “One of the main barriers to turning knowledge into action is the tendency to treat talking about something as equivalent to actually doing something about it.” That quote has stayed with me 66 Whenever we find ourselves debating labels, perhaps we should pause and ask: how does this help people live healthier lives? because it captures something I occasionally feel in our sector. It is not that we lack knowledge. It is that we sometimes confuse the act of naming and describing something with the work of actually addressing it. We are exceptionally good at talking about sanitation. But are we equally as good at staying focused on the fundamental purpose of sanitation? Are we always clear about which transmission pathways we are trying to interrupt? Are we asking whether our interventions genuinely improve people’s lives? Or are we increasingly occupied with debating labels while the underlying public health challenge remains unchanged? Starting with the problem rather than the technology One of the great strengths of Bradley’s framework was that it forced us to begin with the problem rather than the solution. Whether the issue was contaminated drinking water, inadequate access for hygiene or vector-related disease, the intervention followed the diagnosis. The framework forced practitioners to think about function rather than infrastructure. Toilets, sewers and treatment plants were never objectives in themselves; they were simply the means of improving public health outcomes. Perhaps this sounds obvious, but increasingly I wonder whether we still consciously apply this way of thinking. Today many of our discussions begin with technologies, service models or categories of sanitation. We discuss onsite sanitation, faecal sludge management, non-sewered sanitation, water-efficient sanitation, circular sanitation, regenerative sanitation and next-generation sanitation systems. These concepts did not emerge without reason. Faecal sludge management highlighted a critical gap in our understanding of what happens after containment. It forced us to think about collection, Sanitation Sanitation transport, treatment and disposal. Water-efficient sanitation responded to legitimate concerns about water scarcity. Many of these concepts have undoubtedly contributed positively to the sector. The issue is not that they exist. The issue is whether we have become more focused on the terminology than on the purpose. When labels become identities Over time, labels can become more than descriptive tools. They can become identities. They can become communities of practice. They can become movements, with funding streams, conferences and professional networks forming around them. None of this is necessarily bad. The challenge comes when discussions begin to revolve around defending or promoting particular labels rather than solving the underlying problem. Sometimes it feels as though we spend considerable energy debating whether a sanitation system should be described as one thing or another, while paying less attention to whether it is actually reducing exposure to pathogens. At times I sit in these discussions with a heavy heart. Not because I oppose innovation, or because I resist change, but because Ioccasionally wonder whether we are mistaking movement for progress. The uncomfortable reality is that pathogens do not care what terminology we use. They do not attend conferences. They do not read strategic plans. They do not recognise donor priorities. And they do not care whether a project is categorised as onsite sanitation, faecal sludge management, non-sewered sanitation, water-efficient sanitation or next-generation sanitation. They care only about one thing: whether the pathways through which they spread remain open. Isee no reason why experts in faecal sludge management, non-sewered sanitation, water-efficient sanitation, citywide inclusive sanitation and other approaches should not each contribute something valuable. They are often looking at different dimensions of the same challenge: service chains, infrastructure choices, resource efficiency, planning and governance. When that happens, terminology begins behaving less like a tool for understanding and more like a competing ideology. Personally, I find this troubling because public health problems rarely yield to a single lens. Disease transmission is complex. Human. behaviour is complex. And service delivery is complex. So, it seems unlikely that any single label, however useful, could fully capture all of these dimensions. Perhaps our goal should not be to determine which terminology wins, but to understand what each perspective contributes to the larger objective of improving health and quality of life. Have the new labels added value? How should we judge whether a new sanitation concept or label has genuinely added value? Some undoubtedly have. Faecal sludge management is perhaps the clearest example. It did not simply introduce new language it directed attention, resources and technical effort toward a part of the sanitation ~ chain that had been systematically neglected. It changed what practitioners looked at, what funders prioritised, and, ultimately, what got built. The terminology mattered because the practice changed. That, perhaps, is the test for any new concept. Does it help us understand the problem better? Does it help us develop better solutions? And does it improve our ability to reduce exposure and improve people’s quality of life? Or does it primarily change the language 66 I occasionally wonder whether we are mistaking movement for progress we use to describe the problem? Tam aware that others would argue, reasonably, that terminological clarity is often the precondition for practical progress, in that without a shared language, resources cannot be aligned, policies cannot be written and practitioners cannot collaborate. I do not dismiss that argument. But clarity of language and clarity of purpose are not the same thing. The question is whether the new language has moved us closer to the latter. These questions matter because the fundamental challenge has not changed. How do we safely separate people from pathogens, interrupt disease transmission and improve quality of life? They existed before the latest terminology arrived, and they will remain long after it has been replaced by whatever comes next. My concern is not that new ideas emerge. It is that we sometimes confuse the emergence of new language with the achievement of new understanding. When that happens, there is a risk that we create conceptual complexity faster than practical progress. Returning to first principles None of this is an argument against innovation — it remains essential. The challenges facing sanitation today are enormous. Urbanisation, climate change, water scarcity, resource constraints and inequality all demand new thinking. The point is that innovation should not require abandoning the principles that justified sanitation interventions in the first place. So, whenever a new concept, framework or technology emerges, perhaps we should ask a few simple questions. Which transmission routes does it interrupt? Which unintended consequences might it create? Does it improve people’s quality of life? If we cannot clearly answer those questions, there is a risk that our language is evolving faster than our practice. The value of Bradley’s framework was not that it provided all the answers. It was that it forced us to begin with first principles, to start with disease transmission and work towards interventions. Increasingly, I worry that we sometimes do the opposite. We start with technologies, concepts and labels and work backwards. The difference may seem subtle. But it matters. Asimple reminder Perhaps what I am advocating is simply this: that we remember why we do what we do. Sanitation is not fundamentally about technologies, systems or labels. These things matter, but they are not the objective. The objective remains what it has always been: reducing exposure, protecting health and improving quality of life. Everything else is a means to that end. As a student, that lesson was repeatedly drilled into me by a professor who wanted his students to think like public health engineers rather than simply infrastructure designers. Looking back, I am increasingly grateful for that lesson. Because in a world of ever-expanding terminology, it continues to provide a remarkably simple test. Whenever we find ourselves debating labels, perhaps we should pause and ask: how does this help people live healthier lives? I would say that this is my challenge to all of us, particularly to the young water professionals who will help shape the future of our sector. You bring the innovation and ambition the sector needs; my hope is that you pair it with these enduring first principles. The next time we encounter a new concept, framework or piece of terminology, let us be curious. Let us explore what it can teach us. Let us remain open to new ideas and new perspectives. But let us also have the discipline and the courage to return to first principles and ask what problem we are trying to solve, which transmission. pathways we are trying to interrupt, and how the idea before us will actually improve people’s lives. Not in theory. In practice. For real people. If we can do that, then perhaps the many different perspectives within our sector need not compete with one another. Instead, they can become complementary lenses that help us better understand a complex challenge and ultimately achieve the objective that has always mattered most: improving the health, dignity and quality of life of the people we serve.
